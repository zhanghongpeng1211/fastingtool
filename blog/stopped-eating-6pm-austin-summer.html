Why I Stopped Eating at 6 PM During Austin's 110°F Summer (And What It Did to My Sleep)

Sarah Patel · 2024-08-08

Last August, I made a decision that seemed logical at the time. I moved my eating eating-window-planner from noon to 6 PM to 8 AM to 2 PM. The logic was simple: Austin was in the middle of a heat wave. 105, 107, even 110 on some days. I wanted to eat when I was most active and when the sun was at its peak, thinking my metabolism would match the sun's intensity. What I didn't expect was how much that tiny shift would wreck my sleep.

I had been doing intermittent fasting for over a year. My old eating-window-planner was noon to 6 PM. Dinner at 6, maybe a snack at 7, then nothing until lunch the next day. It worked fine. My sleep was good. My energy was steady. But the summer heat was brutal. I would come home from a run, eat dinner, and then try to sleep in a house that wouldn't drop below 84°F until midnight. I thought moving my eating earlier would help my body cool down faster. Turns out, my body didn't agree.

The first week of the new eating-window-planner was rough. I ate breakfast at 8 AM, lunch at noon, and was done by 2 PM. By 5 PM, my stomach was empty. By 8 PM, I was ravenous. By 10 PM, my brain was screaming for food and my body temperature was way up. I would lie in bed, sweating, my mind racing, watching the clock tick past midnight. My sleep fasting-session-tracker showed I was waking up three or four times a night. My deep sleep dropped by 40%.

I couldn't figure out why. I was eating the same amount of food, just earlier. But the timing mattered more than I thought. When you eat, your body generates heat through digestion. That's called diet‑induced thermogenesis. It's the metabolic-analyzer cost of processing food. It's normal and healthy, but if you eat right before bed, that heat can keep you awake. So I thought, if I eat earlier, the heat will dissipate before sleep. That part was correct. But I didn't account for the fact that an empty stomach triggers the release of cortisol, the stress hormone. Cortisol keeps you alert. It's the opposite of what you want at bedtime.

So I was trading one problem for another. Late eating made me hot. Early eating made me hungry and stressed. Neither was good for sleep.

By day five, I was a mess. I was tired, cranky, and my fasting schedule felt impossible. I was counting hours until 8 AM the next morning. That's not how fasting is supposed to feel. It's supposed to feel natural, like your body is on a rhythm. Mine was off.

I started looking at the research. Circadian rhythm fasting is the idea that your body processes food differently at different times of day. Your insulin sensitivity is higher in the morning. Your digestive enzymes are more active in the afternoon. Your melatonin starts rising in the evening, and eating late can disrupt that. The optimal eating eating-window-planner for most people is somewhere between 10 AM and 6 PM. That aligns with the sun's cycle and your body's natural rhythms.

But Austin in August is not natural. The sun is up at 6:30 AM and doesn't set until 8:30 PM. The heat peaks at 3 PM and stays high until 8 PM. Your body is trying to cool down while the air conditioning is trying to catch up. It's a mismatch. So the standard advice doesn't always apply.

What I eventually landed on was a compromise. I moved my eating eating-window-planner to 11 AM to 5 PM. Still earlier than my winter eating-window-planner, but not as early as 8 AM. I ate my last meal at 5 PM, which gave my body four hours to process before sleep. I also added electrolytes to my water in the evening to help with the heat stress. And I started taking a cold shower before bed, which lowered my core temperature quickly.

The cold shower was the game changer. I would stand under cold water for one minute, just enough to drop my skin temperature. That triggered a relaxation response that made my body shift from stress mode to recovery mode. I also started doing deep breathing in bed, four seconds in, six seconds out. That lowered my heart rate and signaled my nervous system to calm down.

Within a few days of this new protocol, my sleep improved. My deep sleep returned to normal. My heart rate variability, which is a measure of stress recovery, increased by 15%. I was waking up feeling rested instead of like I had been hit by a truck. And the hunger at night disappeared. I think my body had adjusted to the new schedule, but I also think the cold shower tricked my system into thinking I was eating. It sounds weird, but temperature regulation and hunger cues are linked. Cooling your body can reduce the desire to eat.

I also learned something about Austin's heat and fasting. When it's that hot, you lose electrolytes faster, even if you're not exercising. You sweat just sitting still. So you need to add salt to your water or you'll feel weak and dizzy. I started adding a pinch of sea salt to my morning water. The difference was immediate. No more afternoon headaches, no more shaky hands.

The other thing I changed was my exercise timing. I used to run at noon because I liked the heat. Bad idea. I started running at 7 AM, before the sun got brutal. That kept my core temperature lower throughout the day. And I stopped eating after my runs. I used to have a post‑run snack, but that was messing with my fasting eating-window-planner. I moved that snack to my lunch meal.

So what's the lesson? Don't blindly follow a fasting schedule that works in winter and expect it to work in summer. Your body is different when it's 110 degrees. It needs more electrolytes, less intense exercise, and a different eating eating-window-planner. And sleep should be your priority. If your sleep is suffering, your fasting schedule is wrong for you.

I'm still fasting. I still love it. But I'm more flexible now. Some days I eat at 11 AM. Some days I eat at 1 PM. The goal is not to be perfect. It's to be consistent while adapting to your environment. Austin summers are an environment. You have to work with them, not against them.

My friend who kept the noon‑to‑6 eating-window-planner through the summer? He told me he slept terribly and felt bloated. He switched to 11‑5 after hearing about my experience. He said the difference was night and day. Literally.

The timing of your meals matters. The quality of your food matters. But the timing of your sleep matters most. If you're fasting but you're not sleeping, you're not doing your body any favors.

— Maya Chen, Austin

P.S. I still get hungry at 8 PM sometimes. I drink sparkling water. It fills my stomach and keeps my hands busy. Works 80% of the time.
